Round 3c — scrim opacity

The proportional rule is settled and unchanged. The only variable here is the scrim's alpha. Round 1 settled that the scrim is a panel with a hairline rather than a wash — a label laid on the card. The 90% figure is a leftover from when the element was a gradient, and 10% of the artwork is still coming through it.

This bar is 150 px — one card at 9-pocket. If it does not match, your zoom is not 100%.

Crosby is the card that ghosts: his own nameplate — Sidney Crosby / Penguins · Center — sits directly under the panel. At 2× the leak is twice the size, so the doubled name becomes readable. This is the case the change exists for.

3 — The counter: does full opacity read as a hole punched in the card?

Some leak may be what sells the panel as sitting on the artwork rather than being cut out of it. A dark card is where a fully opaque near-black panel is most likely to stop reading as a label — the hairline is the only thing separating them. If this is what you see, the answer is a middle value in both layouts, not a per-layout rule.

This is the mild version of the risk, not the worst one. A dark panel on a dark card has barely any contrast across its own boundary, so it cannot read as a hole in either direction — the case is mild because the two things are similar. A hole reads as a hole when there is something bright around it. Section 4 is the real test.

4 — The real hole test: a light-bordered card

Durant, white border, L 0.58–0.63 — the brightest surround in the set. A solid dark rectangle cut into bright artwork at maximum edge contrast is the picture of a window punched through a card. Same set name as sections 1 and 2 so the only variable is the card's brightness — it is a stress test, not Durant's actual set.
